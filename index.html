01 / About

Hardware-first, by choice

I'm completing a BEng in Computer and Electronic Engineering (NQF 8) at North-West University, Potchefstroom. The degree covers both software and hardware. I used my final year and my practical work to choose a direction on purpose, and every project and placement pointed the same way: industrial and hardware systems.

I get the most satisfaction from things I can see and measure. That might be a signal on an oscilloscope, a motor's vibration signature, a PWM duty cycle reacting to a load disturbance, or a network link that either connects or doesn't. Industrial systems fail in ways that matter right away, and finding and preventing those failures is the work I want my career to be about.

I'm looking for a graduate role in telecommunications, embedded & instrumentation engineering, or industrial control & automation, ideally somewhere I can learn from experienced engineers while contributing from early on.

BEng mini-dissertation · NWU · 2026

Android-Based Condition Monitoring for Induction Motors

Real-time, vibration-based condition monitoring of induction motors at grain silo facilities, running fully on an Android device with no internet connectivity or backend infrastructure.

How it works

  • JPCCED-HI (Joint Principal Component Cumulative Empirical Distribution Health Index) reduces a motor's vibration signature to a single health score between 0 and 1.
  • It extracts 18 time- and frequency-domain features and applies PCA to classify motor condition and diagnose one of seven failure modes.
  • A hybrid Kotlin + C++ (NDK) architecture runs the heavy DSP (FFT, Hann windowing, feature extraction) in C++, achieving 290–665 ms execution on mid-range hardware.

Why this method

It only needs data from healthy machines to build a baseline. That matters because most industrial sites, including the target facility, don't have a library of past failures.

Status

Next comes physical lab validation against real motor vibration data, alongside a formal engineering report covering the literature study, design, implementation and evaluation.

 Control Theory II practical · 2025 · Module 69%

PI-Controlled DC-DC Buck-Boost Converter

I designed, simulated, built and tested a closed-loop buck-boost converter that holds a stable output voltage through sudden short-circuit disturbances.

  • Characterised the hardware components, then built and validated a MATLAB Simscape model.
  • Derived the small-signal transfer function and designed a PI controller from it.
  • Implemented the controller on an Arduino Nano in embedded C: PWM generation, ADC voltage feedback with digital filtering, and fault protection.
  • Hardware design included voltage dividers, RC anti-aliasing filters and Zener protection.
  • In testing, I induced a short-circuit load drop (30 V → 15 V). The controller adjusted the duty cycle and restored the setpoint without excessive overshoot.

What I valued most was connecting control theory directly to real hardware behaviour.

 Computer Engineering Design · NWU · 2025

Dual-Axis Hoist Controller & Telemetry (STM32)

For our Computer Engineering Design module, I designed and built the main controller board and firmware for an automated hoist system. The hoist had to move in two directions (side-to-side along a rail and vertical lift), respond to a handheld wireless remote, and constantly stream back weight and height data.

What I built

  • Two-axis motor drive: Configured STM32 hardware timers for PWM speed control and GPIO direction switching on both motors, with limit switches on each axis to prevent mechanical overrun.
  • Wireless remote control: Decoded commands from a handheld remote over an interrupt-driven serial receiver to manage movement and trigger an instant emergency stop.
  • Live telemetry & instrumentation: Sampled an analog load cell through the STM32's ADC to measure suspended weight, read a height sensor to track lift position, and streamed both readings back over serial for operator monitoring.
  • Firmware architecture: Wrote the firmware in C using an interrupt-driven state machine so time-critical tasks (limit checks and PWM updates) never got blocked by telemetry or serial operations.

What I took away

Moving from basic 8-bit chips to a 32-bit ARM Cortex-M micro was a big step up. Getting PWM motor driving, ADC sensor acquisition, and wireless serial communication running smoothly together taught me a lot about timer peripherals, interrupts, and practical hardware debugging on the bench.
